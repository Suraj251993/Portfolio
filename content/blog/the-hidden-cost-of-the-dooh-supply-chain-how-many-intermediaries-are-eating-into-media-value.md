The Hidden Cost of the DOOH Supply Chain: How Many Intermediaries Are Eating Into Media Value?

Programmatic DOOH promises efficiency, flexibility and transparency. But as more platforms enter the transaction, one important question deserves attention: how much of an advertiser’s investment actually reaches the media owner and delivers value on the screen?

The growth of Digital Out-of-Home (DOOH) has changed the way brands plan and buy outdoor advertising. Campaigns that once required lengthy negotiations, manual bookings and direct coordination with media owners can now be planned, activated and managed through programmatic platforms.

It is a significant step forward for the industry.

But there is another side to this development that deserves a closer look.

Behind a programmatic DOOH campaign, there may be several businesses involved in moving a single advertising transaction from the buyer to the screen. DSPs, SSPs, exchanges, resellers, technology providers and media owners can all play a role.

Each participant may provide a useful service. The question is whether the combined cost of these services is creating enough additional value for advertisers and media owners.

More importantly, do advertisers actually know where their campaign budget goes?

The supply chain is becoming more complex

Let’s consider a simplified programmatic DOOH transaction.

An advertiser wants to run a campaign across digital screens in shopping malls, airports or high-traffic urban locations. The agency plans the campaign and uses a Demand-Side Platform (DSP) to identify suitable inventory.

The DSP connects with an SSP or an exchange, which provides access to inventory from media owners. In some cases, resellers or additional technology partners may also participate before the campaign reaches the actual screen network.

The basic transaction looks something like this:

Advertiser → Agency → DSP → Exchange / SSP → Reseller (where applicable) → Media Owner → Digital Screen

This is an illustrative supply-chain model, not a mandatory sequence. Some platforms combine multiple functions, some transactions take place through direct integrations, and others involve private marketplace agreements.

That distinction matters.

The number of companies involved does not automatically determine whether a transaction is inefficient. A technology provider that improves inventory discovery, campaign delivery, targeting or reporting can create genuine value.

The problem begins when buyers cannot clearly understand what each participant contributes and what they charge for it.

Who receives what from the advertising budget?

This is where the conversation becomes commercially important.

When an advertiser allocates a budget to a programmatic DOOH campaign, that amount may cover more than the media itself.

Depending on the buying arrangement, costs can include:

* Agency planning and management fees.
* DSP technology or transaction fees.
* SSP or exchange fees.
* Reseller margins or service charges.
* Data and audience targeting costs.
* Creative adaptation and campaign management.
* Measurement, verification and reporting services.
* The actual media inventory cost.

Not every campaign includes all these costs. Some are bundled into platform pricing, some are charged separately, and some may be negotiated as part of a wider commercial agreement.

However, without a clear breakdown, advertisers may struggle to distinguish the cost of buying media from the cost of accessing the technology and services around it.

For example, consider a hypothetical campaign with an advertiser investment of $100,000.

If $15,000 is allocated to agency and technology services, $85,000 remains for media and other contracted costs. This is only an illustration, not an industry benchmark or a claim about actual DOOH fee rates.

The important question is not whether $15,000 is too much or too little.

It is whether the advertiser knows what that amount covers, whether the services are delivering measurable value, and whether the media owner receives the amount agreed for the inventory.

A lower technology fee is not necessarily better if it comes with poor inventory quality, limited reporting or additional operational work.

Equally, a higher fee should have a clear business justification.

The real problem: Limited visibility into the transaction

Programmatic advertising has spent years developing tools to improve supply-chain transparency.

In the wider digital advertising ecosystem, IAB Tech Lab introduced initiatives such as ads.txt, sellers.json and the SupplyChain object to help buyers identify authorised sellers and understand which intermediaries participate in a transaction.

These are important developments.

However, DOOH has its own operational characteristics. Screen inventory, location data, play schedules, audience estimates and media-owner integrations introduce requirements that are not always identical to those of online display advertising.

The industry therefore needs to go beyond simply adopting digital advertising terminology.

It needs transaction-level visibility that works for the DOOH environment.

Consider some practical questions an advertiser should be able to answer:

* Which media owner supplied the inventory?
* Which screens and locations were included?
* Was the inventory purchased directly or through a reseller?
* Which DSP and SSP participated in the transaction?
* What technology or service fees were applied?
* What was the agreed media cost?
* How many plays were delivered against the campaign?
* What reporting or verification was provided?

If these questions cannot be answered consistently, it becomes difficult to evaluate the true efficiency of a campaign.

And when advertisers cannot compare the total cost against the actual delivery, it becomes harder to make informed buying decisions.

What does the market data tell us?

The commercial discussion is becoming more relevant as programmatic DOOH grows.

The World Out of Home Organization’s (WOO) inaugural Global pDOOH Expenditure Study 2026 reported that global programmatic DOOH expenditure reached approximately $1.339 billion in 2025, representing 7% of total global DOOH expenditure.

The study collected confidential revenue data from 12 SSP platforms across more than 40 markets, with PwC independently aggregating the submissions.

One finding deserves particular attention: private marketplaces accounted for 75.7% of global pDOOH expenditure, while open auction represented a much smaller share.

This tells us something important about how the market operates.

Despite the programmatic label, a significant part of pDOOH is still traded through structured agreements between selected buyers and sellers rather than through fully open auctions.

There is nothing inherently wrong with private marketplaces. They can offer buyers greater control over inventory selection, pricing, availability and brand suitability.

But they also make it important to understand the commercial terms behind each transaction.

A private marketplace should not mean that the buyer has less visibility into the costs or the supply path.

There is another important limitation worth acknowledging: WOO’s study measures aggregate programmatic DOOH expenditure. It does not provide a complete transaction-level breakdown of advertiser spend, DSP fees, SSP fees, reseller margins and net media-owner receipts.

Therefore, it would be incorrect to use the $1.339 billion figure to calculate how much intermediaries earn.

The industry needs more detailed commercial data before that question can be answered accurately.

Are intermediaries creating value or simply adding cost?

This is probably the most important question for the entire ecosystem.

Intermediaries are not necessarily the problem.

A DSP can simplify campaign planning and provide access to multiple inventory sources. An SSP can help media owners make their inventory available to a wider group of buyers. An exchange can support transaction management and connections between demand and supply.

Resellers can also add value when they provide access to otherwise difficult-to-reach inventory, local market expertise or campaign execution support.

These services have a legitimate place in the ecosystem.

However, the industry should examine situations where multiple platforms perform overlapping functions, where inventory passes through several commercial relationships without a clear benefit, or where the buyer cannot identify the actual source of supply.

For example, if a buyer can access the same screen inventory through two different supply paths, the comparison should not stop at the quoted media price.

It should also consider:

* Total technology and service costs.
* Inventory availability and quality.
* Actual campaign delivery.
* Reporting accuracy.
* Operational complexity.
* The level of control and transparency provided.

The most efficient supply path is not necessarily the one with the fewest participants. It is the one that delivers the required inventory and service at a justified total cost.

What needs to change? Five practical steps towards a more transparent ecosystem

The industry does not need to remove every intermediary. It needs to make every participant accountable for the value they contribute.

1. Introduce a clear cost breakdown

Every programmatic DOOH campaign should provide a commercial breakdown that distinguishes media cost, technology fees, agency services, data costs and other applicable charges.

Where commercial confidentiality prevents disclosure of individual supplier margins, buyers should still receive enough information to understand the total cost structure.

2. Make the supply path visible

Buyers should be able to identify the media owner, authorised seller, DSP, SSP and any additional reseller involved in the transaction.

Existing IAB Tech Lab transparency concepts, including sellers.json and the SupplyChain object, offer useful foundations. The DOOH industry should work towards practical implementation suited to its own inventory and transaction models.

3. Connect financial reporting with delivery reporting

A campaign report should not only show impressions, plays or screen locations.

It should also help the advertiser connect the agreed media investment with actual campaign delivery.

Financial reconciliation and proof of play should be treated as connected parts of campaign accountability.

4. Encourage direct integrations and smarter supply-path optimisation

Media owners and technology providers should explore integrations that reduce unnecessary duplication and operational overhead.

DSPs and agencies should compare available supply paths based on total cost, inventory quality, delivery performance and reporting capabilities rather than simply choosing the cheapest route.

5. Establish common commercial and reporting standards

Industry associations, media owners, DSPs, SSPs and agencies should work towards common definitions for media spend, platform fees, transaction costs, delivery and reporting.

Without common definitions, even a well-intentioned transparency initiative can produce inconsistent results.

What can the industry do next?

The responsibility is shared.

Advertisers and agencies should ask for a clear cost breakdown, request supply-path information and compare the actual value delivered across different buying routes.

DSPs and SSPs should make their commercial models easier to understand and clearly explain the services included in their fees.

Media owners should understand how their inventory is being distributed, who is authorised to sell it and how campaign revenue is reconciled.

Industry associations should encourage shared reporting standards, better transaction visibility and wider participation in independent market research.

Technology providers should also consider developing reporting systems that allow buyers to connect campaign expenditure, transaction participants, inventory delivery and campaign performance in one place.

This would make transparency part of the product itself rather than an additional reporting exercise.

The future: Fewer unnecessary costs, more measurable value

The next phase of programmatic DOOH will not be defined only by the number of connected screens, DSP integrations or available marketplaces.

It will also be defined by how efficiently the ecosystem connects advertisers with media owners.

Artificial intelligence and automation may make campaign planning, inventory discovery, pricing and optimisation faster. But automation will not automatically make the supply chain transparent.

If the underlying transaction data is fragmented, an automated system may simply process that complexity faster.

The opportunity is to build a more connected ecosystem where buyers can understand the supply path, compare the total cost, validate delivery and evaluate the contribution of every technology partner.

That does not mean every intermediary needs to disappear.

It means every intermediary should be able to answer a simple question:

What measurable value are you adding to this transaction?

The question the industry should be asking

Programmatic DOOH has made outdoor advertising more accessible, flexible and measurable. That progress should not be underestimated.

But as the ecosystem grows, the industry must be willing to examine its own commercial structure.

Advertisers deserve to understand where their investment goes. Media owners deserve a fair and transparent return for the inventory they provide. Technology providers deserve to be paid for the value they create.

These interests do not have to compete.

The future of programmatic DOOH should not be about adding more platforms between the advertiser and the screen. It should be about building a supply chain where every participant contributes something meaningful, every cost has a clear purpose, and every transaction can be properly understood.

Because ultimately, the success of programmatic DOOH should not be measured only by how much money enters the ecosystem.

It should also be measured by how much value the ecosystem creates.
